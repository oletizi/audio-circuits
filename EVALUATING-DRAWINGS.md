# Evaluating drawings

**Canonical. Referenced from `CONSTITUTION.md` and `CLAUDE.md`.** This document
says who may evaluate a drawing, what counts as a valid evaluation, and what an
agent must do when it has produced one. It exists so the owner can point at it
instead of repeating himself.

**A drawing means any rendered artifact a person is meant to read** — a
schematic page, a board layout, a stripboard plan.

---

## The failure that earned this document

The owner, 2026-10-28:

> *"This has been an iron constant: you fail to ask me to evaluate drawings no
> matter how stridently I DEMAND to be the only qualified judge. I'm tired of
> asking because it has heretofore been an exercise in futility."*

On a single day an agent sent him rendered pages on six occasions and asked for
his judgement on none of them, while ranking tools by drawing quality in two
committed governance documents and writing *"best page produced by anything"*
into one. It had conceded the rule four times that same day, once in the message
immediately before breaking it again.

> *"you are falling into the trap of presupposing what makes for a legible
> drawing when, in fact, you have no eyes."*

**Telling an agent not to do this has been tried and does not work.** The rules
below are therefore written so that compliance is checkable from the outside,
and so that an agent's opinion changes no state even when it is offered.

# The principles

**These sit above the numbered rules below.** The rules say who may judge a
drawing and how one is handled. The principles say what makes a drawing worth
reading, and they are general: they are **not** findings about any one page,
any one circuit or any one tool, and must never be filed as such.

**There are THREE kinds of statement in this document and they bind
differently** - mistaking one for another is how its worst clauses got written:

| | what it is | how it binds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The principles** (`P1`-`P4`) | what makes a drawing worth reading | they bind; an agent applies them and may be held to them |
| **The style preferences** (`S1`-) | conventions that pay off by accumulating across drawings | **followed, never enforced** - no gate, no score, and never a reason to withhold a page |
| **The numbered rules** (1-9) | who may judge a drawing and how one is handled | they bind absolutely, and several are written to be checkable from outside |

**Every one was stated by the owner while looking at a rendered page, and every
one is quoted in his words.** None was authored by an agent. The only clause in
this document an agent did author - *"a hairpin earns its place never"* - was
struck within the hour as unearned certainty, which is the clearest evidence
available for why the authorship rule exists.

## P1. A device is noise unless it is earning its place

**And the draughtsman must be able to say what work it is doing.** A device that
cannot be justified is not neutral. In the owner's words: *"that's just
littering."*

This is the general form. The four below are instances of it, not separate
rules, and the list is **open** - a fifth device found tomorrow is judged by P1,
not by whether it appears here.

### Noise: a device is noise unless it is earning its place

**Four criteria, stated by the owner on 2026-10-29 and promoted here at his
instruction: *"these are all criteria worth promoting"*.** They share one
form - **each device is clarity or noise depending on whether it is doing
work, and the draughtsman must be able to say what work.** A device that
cannot be justified is not neutral. In his words: *"that's just littering."*

**A label.** **Collapse duplicate on-page labels into a wire. Always, without
reasoning about it.** The owner, 2026-10-29:

> *"Rather than try to come up with an exquisite line of reasoning about when
> duplicate on-page labels is good or bad, you should just assume you need to
> collapse them. I will tell you when I want you to use on-page labels."*

**That is the whole rule.** There is no test to apply, no threshold, and no
defence to weigh. An on-page label that duplicates another is collapsed; an
on-page label is used when the reviewer asks for one. Off-page references are
not what this is about.

The reasoning behind it, in his words, kept as context and not as a procedure:

> *"Labels are for off-page references or for distant symbols on a crowded page
> where the connecting wire would be hard to trace by eye. For parts right next
> to each other, labels instead of wires creates unnecessary noise and cognitive
> load. It's also bad style."*
>
> *"the long rail is the right choice. It's much more clear than a pair of
> labels would have been. On-page labels in place of wires should be used very
> sparingly. I've almost never seen it used."*
>
> *"on-page label duplication is probably an indicator of something wrong."*

> **THREE DRAFTS OF THIS CLAUSE WERE STRUCK IN ONE DAY, ALL BY THE SAME AGENT,
> AND THE PATTERN IS THE POINT.** The first claimed *"a hairpin earns its place
> never"* - an absolute from an observation. The second claimed *"when they
> conflict, the wire wins"* - an ordering law from a single verdict. The third
> was an elaborate test for when duplication is defensible, which the owner
> struck as *"over-thinking the rules again"* and *"read[ing] the tea leaves."*
> Each time a verdict on an instance was promoted into a decision procedure.
> **The lamp-post problem reaches for the codifiable thing, and a rule is the
> most codifiable thing there is.** Where the reviewer has given a default,
> apply the default and stop.

**A repeated ground symbol** earns its place by shortening a run.

> *"Multiple ground symbols aid clarity when they reduce long wire runs on a
> crowded page. They are just noise when stacked on top of each other. Also,
> bad style."*

**A long wire run** earns its place by separating things that are semantically
apart, or by relieving crowding.

> *"Unnecessarily long wire runs that aren't used for any good reason like
> reducing crowding are noisy and bad style. It's harder to trace long wire runs
> than shorter runs - although sometimes part separation for semantic reasons
> with long runs can aid comprehension. But, long, crooked runs need a *reason*,
> not just a *shrug*. That's just littering."*

**A hairpin earns its place by defence**, and the prior is against it:

> *"the hairpin ground is also noisy and bad style - stemming from poor part
> orientation or placement. Hairpins in general are an indicator of something
> amiss with symbol organization, orientation, and/or placement."*

> *"There *may* be instances where hairpins are necessary or *are* a problem
> with wire placement - though I would wager its more likely caused by careless
> part organization, organization, or placement. Hairpins demand a defense; they
> don't get to stay on the drawing 'just because that's where we happened to
> drop our bag of parts.'"*

**So look upstream of the wire first**, because that is where the cause usually
is - a part facing the wrong way or sitting in the wrong place. A hairpin
straightened without touching the orientation that produced it has treated the
symptom and discarded the diagnosis. But *usually* is not *always*: a hairpin
whose cause genuinely is the routing, or which is genuinely necessary, may
stay - **if someone can say why.** What it may not do is stay unexamined.

> **An earlier draft of this clause read "A hairpin earns its place never."**
> The owner struck it on 2026-10-29: *"be careful with strict ultimata -
> 'hairpins are never…' is unearned certainty and false precision."* It is shown
> struck rather than edited away because the error is the one this project keeps
> making - an agent took an observation that a thing is *usually* a symptom and
> wrote it down as a law, which is a stronger claim than the evidence carries
> and than the reviewer made.

**And littering is evidence about the process, not only about the page.** The
owner, 2026-10-29: *"That kind of littering is a sign of carelessness."* Any one
stray label or stacked ground symbol is minor on its own - which is why they are
easy to wave through. What they indicate collectively is a drawing that was
emitted and not attended to. **That is why these criteria are worth enforcing
even though no single instance of one makes a drawing unreadable.**

**Two of these are structural in at least one tool and will recur.** copperhead
emits a label wherever a net spans more than one group or carries more than four
endpoints regardless of proximity, and emits one ground symbol per pin on a
ground-class net with no horizontal-rail primitive at all. A drafting pass
behind it has to put the wires back and collapse the grounds.


## P2. Littering is evidence about the process, not only about the page

Any one stray label or stacked ground symbol is minor on its own, which is why
each is easy to wave through. **What they indicate collectively is a drawing
that was emitted and not attended to.** That is why P1 is enforced even though
no single breach of it makes a drawing unreadable.

## P3. Take the principles from a reference, never its proportions

A reference drawing is a scaffold for the draughtsman, not a specification for
the drawing. Signal flow, a ground rail, proximity meaning relatedness - those
transfer. Sheet size, span and spacing do not, and copying them is the
lamp-post problem wearing a reference's clothes: **the copyable part absorbs the
attention the hard part needed.**

## P4. Whenever a task offers something concrete to copy or count, expect the
## concrete thing to absorb the attention the hard thing needed

The general statement of the lamp-post problem, from which P3 follows. Stated
in full under rule 6.

# The style preferences

**These are codified deliberately and at the owner's instruction**, 2026-10-29:

> *"you can codify this as a **style preference** rather than a rule - especially
> if you include the motivation for applying consistent style"*

**The motivation is the condition on which they may be written down at all**, so
it comes first and every entry carries it.

## Why a style preference is worth following, which is the whole of its force

> *"consistent style and a common style convention accumulate to increase
> readability and comprehension - that's much of what style preferences are
> for."* - the owner, 2026-10-29

**A style preference earns its keep by ACCUMULATION, never by any single
application.** The argument for following one is not that this drawing needs it,
because usually it does not - it is that **a reader who has learned where to
look keeps that knowledge across every drawing, and an inconsistent page spends
it.** The benefit is paid to the reader of the next drawing and the one after,
which is exactly why it is invisible in the drawing in front of you.

**This is the same shape as P2, by a different mechanism.** Any one breach is
minor, which is why each is easy to wave through. P2 is about what littering
reveals of the *process*; a style preference is about what consistency buys the
*reader*.

## What a style preference is NOT

**It is not a rule.** The owner, striking an earlier version of this section that
was numbered `P5` and titled as an imperative:

> *"again, don't codify this as a rule - it's a **style** preference"*

**The title was the codification.** That version argued at length against
hardening the convention into an ultimatum while stating it as one in its own
heading, inside the numbered series beside principles that do bind - so an agent
reading only the heading would never have reached the hedge. **A heading is the
part that gets obeyed.**

So, operatively:

- **Follow them.** An agent drawing a page applies them without being asked.
- **Do not enforce them.** No gate, no check that passes or fails, no score, and
  **never a refusal to produce a page.** Rule 3 already says only an electrical
  fault may withhold a drawing, and a style departure is not one.
- **Report a departure as an observation**, in the vocabulary rule 2 allows -
  where the input enters, where the output leaves, which way each points - never
  as a finding that something failed.
- **Do not invent thresholds.** Where the owner has supplied a hedge, the hedge
  is part of the preference and is not to be resolved into a number.
- **An agent does not author one.** Same invariant as the principles: these come
  from the reviewer, looking at a page, in his words.

## S1. Signal flows left to right and top to bottom; an output points right

**Like English text.** An input enters at the left, an output leaves at the
right, pointing right. The owner, 2026-10-29, on the Lo boost stand-in group of
the `pultec-hi-boost` page:

> *"Another way to look at why the lo boost stand-in section is goofy is that
> signal usually flows left to right and top to bottom (like english text). This
> had the input below the output, which is goofy. Also, output is usually
> pointing right, horizontally. You have output pointing up which is exactly
> backwards of convention. In this case, it's not a big deal, since it's such a
> small network, but it's still goofy and definitely bad style."*

**HIS OWN HEDGE IS PART OF S1 AND IS NOT TO BE RESOLVED:** *"not a big deal,
since it's such a small network, but it's still goofy and definitely bad
style."* Worth fixing at any size; not equally urgent at any size. **No clause
says signal must always flow left to right, and no number says how small a small
network is.** Supplying either would be the fourth instance of a pattern this
document already carries three struck examples of - *"a hairpin earns its place
never"*, *"when they conflict, the wire wins"*, and the defensibility test for
labels. Each was a law induced from one verdict.

**S1 also recovers an item P3 had set aside.** P3 lists *"signal flow"* first
among what transfers from a reference drawing. S1 is that item in the owner's
own words rather than inferred from a drawing, which is the difference between a
preference and a reading of one.

## A style preference can reach a cause a principle cannot

**S1 did, which is why this section is not advisory decoration.** A hairpin in
that same group had already been named under P1, and the correction derived from
P1 alone - re-place the grounded part so its ground symbol stops folding back -
was a true repair of a real defect that would have **left the group's input
below its output and its output pointing up.** Read against S1 instead, the
hairpin **dissolves as a consequence rather than as a repair.**

**The test of whether a correction reached the cause is to ask what else it
fixes.** A repair that fixes exactly the one thing pointed at and nothing
adjacent is usually a patch.

## How a style preference is added

The same way a principle is: **the reviewer states it while looking at a
rendered page, and it is recorded in his words, with the motivation, in the
change that captured it.** It gets the next `S` number. An agent that believes it
has noticed a convention has noticed a candidate to put to him, not a preference
to write down.

**They do not graduate to `CONSTITUTION.md`.** The principles may, on evidence
across several drawings; a style preference stays here by its nature, because
what it governs is taste that serves comprehension rather than a failure the
project refuses to repeat.

## How a principle graduates

**A principle here is not yet a commandment, and the difference is evidence.**
The owner, 2026-10-29: *"I want to make sure that these concepts get honed into
high order principles and eventually commandments and not lost as only
applicable to this one drawing."*

`CONSTITUTION.md` holds what this project does not revisit, and every principle
in it names the failure that earned it. A principle here graduates to there when
it has been **applied across several drawings and several circuits** and has
either held or been corrected - not on the strength of the page that prompted
it. The amendment procedure is the constitution's own: quote the clause,
give the evidence, carry the owner's agreement in his words, land it with the
work.

**Until then they bind anyway.** This document is canonical and is cited from
`CONSTITUTION.md` and `CLAUDE.md`. "Not yet a commandment" is a statement about
how much evidence stands behind a principle, not about whether an agent may
ignore it.

## 1. A drawing has no quality until a qualified reviewer says so

> *"a drawing isn't considered good or bad until it has been reviewed by a
> qualified reviewer. At the moment, that means me."*

An unreviewed drawing is not good, not bad, and not acceptable. It is
**unreviewed**, and that is a state, not an absence.

**The agent that produced a drawing is never a qualified reviewer of it.** Nor
is any agent, at present. This is not a permanent claim about agents — see §7.

## 2. The agent reports observations. Never verdicts.

An **observation** is a statement about what is on the page that another reader
could check: *this wire runs from here to there*; *this label sits on this
wire*; *there are N crossings*; *this block's input is on its right*; *the
rotary is drawn with six selector pins and a mounting lug*.

A **verdict** is a claim about quality. **These words are forbidden about a
drawing this project produced**, in any message, document, commit or code
comment:

> better · best · worse · good · bad · clean · readable · legible · clear ·
> tidy · wins · ugly · *I would build from this* · *this reads well*

The ban is on the vocabulary rather than the intent, because an agent producing
a verdict believes it is describing. **If the word cannot be used, the verdict
cannot be smuggled.** Quoting someone else's verdict is permitted and must be
attributed.

## 3. Every drawing is shown. Only an electrical fault may withhold one.

> *"you can't withhold a drawing from me based on an unqualified review or gate
> (except for electrical flaws) and you can't promote a drawing to approved
> without showing me. You always have to show me the drawing and you have to
> track the approval status"*

**May withhold:** a drawing whose connectivity disagrees with the circuit model,
or that fails ERC. There is nothing to evaluate, because the drawing says a
different circuit.

**May never withhold:** a legibility check, a lint count, a score, a metric, an
agent's opinion, or an agent's belief that the drawing is not ready yet.

## 4. A drawing is sent with a request for evaluation and nothing else

**A message carrying a drawing carries a question and nothing else.** No
assessment, no ranking, no "here is what I think is wrong with it" in the same
breath. Observations may follow *after* the reviewer has given a verdict, or on
request.

This is the rule written to be checkable from the outside: **if a message
contains a drawing and an assessment of it, this rule broke.**

Comparing two drawings is the reviewer's act. An agent may place drawings side
by side and must not order them.

## 5. Approval is state the reviewer writes. It is never derived.

No check passing, no clean lint, no absence of findings, and no agent statement
advances a drawing's status. **Status changes only when the reviewer records a
verdict**, and it is bound to the exact artifact reviewed so that a stale
approval cannot be expressed.

An agent may say a drawing is unreviewed. It may not say it is ready.

## 6. What to evaluate, and the trap in what gets measured

> *"Label and wire collisions are not actually that big of a problem. I can
> still understand a schematic with some collisions. The biggest problem is rats
> nests with wires and part placement/orientation that don't make any sense…
> then, it's really hard to make sense of what I'm looking at.*
>
> *Previous efforts have obsessed over text collisions and utterly ignored rats
> nests, hairpins, and non-sensical orientations. I chalk that up to the lamp
> post problem: collisions are easy for an algorithm to detect; rats nests,
> hairpins and orientation goofiness is much harder to quantify so agents ignore
> them because they're hard to pay attention to."*

**Attention follows measurability, and the measurable defects are the minor
ones.** Three invented readability metrics went to text collisions because text
collisions are countable.

**It is wider than metrics: it produces literalism about any concrete, copyable
detail.** Given a reference drawing to work from, an agent reproduced its sheet
size, its full-width spine and its empty regions - the measurable, copyable
part - from a circuit with a quarter of the content. The owner, 2026-10-29:
*"Fixating on a very literal detail is a symptom of autistic literalism that you
have, stemming from the lamp post problem."* **Whenever a task offers something
concrete to copy or count, expect the concrete thing to absorb the attention the
hard thing needed.**

So: **an automated check is peripheral by definition.** It may be run and
reported. It may refuse for an electrical fault (§3). It may never approve, may
never rank, and a page with no findings is not thereby good.

**And the reviewer's eye is not a stand-in for a checker that does not exist
yet.** For the defects that make a drawing unreadable it is the only instrument
there is.

**The target is readability itself, never conformance to a reference.** The
owner, 2026-10-29: *"I care about readability, not idioms... The idiom was
something to give you as a reference, not something I actually care about."* A
reference may be given to an agent as an aid, and a page that follows one
closely is not thereby acceptable, nor is one that follows none thereby
unacceptable. The question put to the reviewer is never "does this match the
reference".

**For what makes a drawing worth reading, see The principles above** - P1 in
particular, which governs every device on a page.

**And see The style preferences, which bind differently.** They are not rules,
are never gated on and may never withhold a page - but they reach faults P1 does
not: a page can satisfy P1 completely and still read **"exactly backwards of
convention"** (`S1`), which is where the Lo boost stand-in group was after a P1
repair had already been derived for it. A style preference earns its keep by
accumulating across drawings, not by any one of them needing it.

**Readability is judged first and is perishable.** It is spent by familiarity —
whoever has read the netlist or placed the parts can no longer see the page the
way its reader will.

## 7. How the reviewer role is extended

> *"don't invent a perverse project-wide rule that I am the only true arbiter.
> For now, I am the only trusted reviewer. As we mature, that will hopefully
> change and more of the reviewing work can be moved to agents"*

**Reviewer is a role, not a person.** It is currently filled by the owner alone.

An agent enters the role by **demonstrated agreement with the recorded
verdicts**, judged by the owner — never by assertion, by a passing suite, or by
its own estimate of its competence. This is why verdicts are recorded: without
that record there is no evidence from which the role could ever be extended.

Published evidence on an adjacent structurally-precise domain reports *"near-zero
agreement with human raters"* for model-as-judge, so the naive form of this is
already known not to work.

## 8. The reviewer says what is wrong, not what to do about it

The reviewer names the defect. The agent derives the correction.

**And the corrected region is the least-checked part of the drawing**, because
an agent applies a correction perfectly and without resistance — the compliance
is what leaves it unexamined. A region changed in response to a verdict is
flagged for the reviewer first on the next round.

## 9. The agent's job is to help the owner decide

> *"Your job is to help me make decisions, not make decisions for me."*

Present the evidence and the options. Name what is checkable. Say plainly what
is not known. **A recommendation is permitted only where it is asked for, and is
never a substitute for the evidence it rests on.**
